There is a new comment doing the rounds in review meetings, Slack threads and document margins. It is rarely written this bluntly, but it means the same thing every time.

"This looks AI-generated."

Notice it is not "this is wrong". It is not even "I disagree". It is a comment about where the work came from, and somehow it lands harder than any technical objection. Once it is said, the document is no longer being read. It is being suspected.

I have been on both ends of this. I have used AI to tidy a design paper and been quietly discounted for it. I have also read a forty-page strategy that was fluent, well headed, beautifully bulleted and completely empty, and my eyebrows did the reviewing before my brain did. So this is not a post about whose side is right. Both sides are right, both sides are guilty, and that is exactly the problem.

It has reached the family WhatsApp group also. Somebody posts a well-written birthday message and within two minutes there is a reply: "Very nice. Who wrote it?"

## Socrates saw this coming

None of this is new, actually. About 2,400 years ago, in Plato's *Phaedrus*, Socrates complained about a dangerous new technology. Writing.

His worry was that written words would give people the appearance of wisdom without the reality of it. Readers would seem to know a great deal while knowing very little. And worse, a written text cannot defend itself. Ask it a question and it just says the same thing again, with a straight face.

Replace "writing" with "AI" and you could post it on LinkedIn tomorrow. It would do numbers.

But Socrates also handed us the answer, almost by accident. If the danger is words that cannot answer for themselves, then the test is simple. Find out whether the person behind the words can.

So there are four people in every review meeting now. Two of them wrote things. Two of them read things.

## The craftsman and the wizard

The first author is the craftsman. She uses AI the way a carpenter uses a power drill. She knows where the hole should go before she picks it up. The model drafts, she argues with it, throws half of it away, and what remains has her fingerprints on every decision. Ask her why option B was rejected and she will talk for twenty minutes, with feeling.

The second author is the Wizard of Oz.

Everybody knows this one. A giant floating head, smoke and flames, a voice like thunder. Very impressive. Very plausible. Then Toto wanders over and pulls back a curtain, and there is an ordinary man from Kansas working the levers. "Pay no attention to that man behind the curtain!"

AI has given everyone a smoke machine. Anyone can now produce a document with the full Emerald City treatment. Headings, trade-off tables, risks and mitigations, all booming in the right places. And the wizard is not always a fraud, mind you. Remember what he says when he is caught: "I'm a very good man. I'm just a very bad wizard." Often he is just busy, or new, or under pressure to look senior. But the effects do not come with the substance. When somebody pulls the curtain, there are only levers.

## The good-faith reviewer is Toto

Now the readers.

The first reviewer engages in good faith, and she is basically Toto. Not angry, not accusing. Just curious about the curtain. Polish neither impresses nor offends her. She asks the questions only an author can answer. What did you reject? What breaks first? Who did you talk to? What would change your mind?

This is Socrates' test in a review meeting, and the beautiful thing is that it is completely fair. The craftsman passes it without effort, because she lived through every decision. The wizard fails it, because he did not. Nobody has to accuse anybody of anything. The curtain does the work.

And notice, the good-faith reviewer never needed to know whether AI was involved. The question simply did not matter. She judged the thinking, not the typing.

## The accuser is honest Iago

The second reviewer skips the questions. And usually he does not accuse you outright either. He hints.

Shakespeare gave us his patron saint. In *Othello*, Iago is passed over for promotion. Othello makes Cassio his lieutenant instead, and Iago never forgives either of them. But he never makes a direct accusation he could be held to. He insinuates. A raised eyebrow, a "I'm sure it's nothing", a handkerchief in the wrong place. Everyone calls him "honest Iago", because he sounds so reasonable, so concerned, so balanced. And bit by bit he poisons the well until the truth no longer gets a hearing.

That is what "this looks AI-generated" becomes when it is used as a verdict rather than a question. Philosophers even have a name for the move: poisoning the well. Discredit the source before anyone hears the argument, and the argument never gets a fair trial. It is seductive, because it costs nothing, sounds wise, and can never be disproven. Nobody can prove how a document was made. You can only prove whether it holds.

Here is the part we rarely say out loud. Very often the accusation has little to do with the document at all. Like Iago, the reviewer is carrying something older. He wanted the role you got. He lost the last argument with you, or the one before that. He was there first, and the old way of doing things was his way. Sometimes he is on his way out and has nothing left to lose. The document is just the nearest available handkerchief.

None of this makes him a villain in real life, mind you. A grudge is usually a wound nobody tended. And sometimes he has simply met too many wizards and is tired of smoke. Very human. Still unfair. The work deserves to be judged on its own, not used as a proxy for an old fight.

## We are all four people

Here is the uncomfortable bit. These are not four kinds of people. They are four moods, and I have been every one of them in the same week.

Monday I am the craftsman, arguing with a model at 10pm about naming conventions. Wednesday, with a deadline breathing on my neck, I paste a draft without reading it twice, and there is a little smoke coming off it. Thursday I ask a junior colleague "walk me through why" out of genuine curiosity, full Toto. Friday, tired, still sore about a decision that went the other way, I glance at a very neat slide deck from the person who won that argument and think "ChatGPT" before I have read the second bullet. Honest Iago, reporting for duty.

## Three rules, not two

If you write: never submit anything you could not defend with the curtain open. If a section came from the model and you cannot explain it, cut it or learn it.

If you review: ask before you accuse. "Walk me through why" costs ten seconds and is fair to everyone. "This looks AI-generated" is a handkerchief with a Slack notification. And before you send it, check honestly whether you are reviewing the document or the person who wrote it.

And one more for authors, because both sides deserve it: if the same person keeps doubting you, ask whether you ever gave them a reason. Iago was a villain, no doubt. But Othello never once sat him down and asked what was wrong.

That is all. No policy document needed (though I am sure someone will write one, and it will look very plausible).

## The answer was always conversation

Socrates never solved the problem of writing by banning it. He did something better. He kept talking to people. Question, answer, another question. Dialogue was his remedy for words that could not defend themselves, and it works just as well for words a machine helped produce. It even works on grudges, given enough patience and a cup of tea.

When reviewers ask real questions, wizards learn quickly that smoke is not enough, and they go and do the actual thinking. When authors can defend every line, and are generous enough to hear out the people who doubt them, the Iagos lose their audience. Trust does not come back by detecting AI. It comes back when people show their thinking, and other people are curious enough to look.

We have been handed the most powerful pen in history. The question was never who held it. The question is who stands behind what it wrote, and who has the grace to ask them before deciding.

So stand behind your work. Ask before you judge. And when someone pulls back the curtain, make sure the person standing there is someone you are proud to be.
